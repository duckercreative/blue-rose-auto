# Steering and Suspension Checks That Belong With Every Brake Inspection

When a car is on the lift for brakes, the wheels are already off and the steering and suspension parts are in plain view. That is the cheapest moment to inspect them.

Every brake inspection should include a quick look at the steering and suspension, because those parts sit right behind the wheel and wear for the same reasons. A worn tie rod end, ball joint or strut changes how the car stops: it can pull to one side, shake under braking, or wear new pads unevenly. Checking them while the wheels are off adds minutes to the job, not hours.

This repository hosts the [Blue Rose Auto Brake Care Guide](https://duckercreative.github.io/blue-rose-auto/brake-inspection-checklist/), a set of plain-language pages on brake inspection, warning signs, pads and rotors, fluid and wet-weather braking. This README covers the part of the front end that brake pages tend to skip.

## What to look at while the wheels are off

| Part | What it does | How it shows wear |
|---|---|---|
| Tie rod ends | connect the steering rack to each wheel | play when the tire is rocked at 9 and 3 o'clock, uneven wear on the tire edges |
| Ball joints | let the wheel pivot and move up and down | play when the tire is rocked at 12 and 6 o'clock, a clunk over bumps |
| Control arm bushings | hold the arms to the frame | cracked or split rubber, a shift under hard braking |
| Struts and shocks | control body motion | oil on the body of the strut, bouncing after bumps, nose-dive when stopping |
| Sway bar links | keep the body flat in corners | a knock on uneven pavement at low speed |
| CV boots | keep grease in the axle joints | splits and grease flung around the inside of the wheel |

None of these are special tests. They are the checks a technician makes with a hand on the tire and a light on the joints.

## Symptoms you notice from the driver's seat

**Pulling when you brake.** If the car drifts only under braking, start with the brakes: a sticking caliper or contaminated pad. If it drifts all the time, look at alignment and worn steering joints.

**Shaking.** A steering wheel that shakes under braking points to rotors. Shaking at steady highway speed, with or without the brakes, points to wheel balance, a bent wheel or worn suspension parts.

**Clunks and knocks.** A single clunk when you first press the brake pedal often comes from a worn control arm bushing letting the arm shift. A knock over speed bumps is usually a sway bar link or ball joint.

**Nose-dive.** If the front of the car drops sharply every time you stop, the front struts are tired. Worn struts let weight shift forward faster, which loads the front tires unevenly and can lengthen stops.

**Wandering on the highway.** Constant small corrections to stay in your lane usually mean play in the steering linkage or an alignment that has drifted.

## Potholes and Lane County roads

Winter rain and freeze cycles open potholes every year, and one hard hit can bend a wheel or knock the alignment out. Triple-I notes that potholes [usually damage wheels and tires, and sometimes the steering, suspension and alignment systems](https://www.iii.org/article/does-my-auto-insurance-cover-damage-caused-by-potholes). It also points out that collision coverage normally pays for pothole damage, but the bill is often smaller than a $500 or $1,000 deductible, so a claim may not be worth filing.

After a bad hit, check three things on the next drive: does the steering wheel sit straight, does the car pull, and does a new vibration appear. Any of the three is a reason for an alignment check. Eugene residents can report the pothole itself through the city's [pothole service request](https://www.eugene-or.gov/620/Potholes).

## How often to check

- At every brake service, since the wheels are already off.
- After any pothole or curb strike hard enough to make you wince.
- When a tire shows uneven wear on its inner or outer edge.
- Once a year on a high-mileage car, before the rainy season.
